Calibration

Which modalities earn their place?

Pre-call volatility does most of the work. Text has standalone signal but adds little incrementally; voice lowers test AUC.

The question

Earnings calls are the densest unstructured signal a public company emits. This project asks a narrow, testable version of a large question: does the way management speaks — and the language they use — forecast how volatile the stock will be after the call?

Data

MAEC (CIKM 2020): 3,443 earnings calls from S&P 1500 companies, each with a transcript and 29 acoustic descriptors per segment. CC BY-SA 4.0; not redistributed here. Prices come from yfinance, with SPY as the market benchmark.

Labels are the top tercile of post-call realised volatility, measured over the five trading days after the call and market-adjusted against SPY. The threshold is learned on the training period only. A post/pre ratio target was tested and rejected because it removes the predictable volatility level.

Protocol

Time split: train 2015–2016, validation 2017 H1, test 2017 H2–2018 H1. No random splits. Company-disjoint cross-validation is the robustness check, because the same firm appears repeatedly. Text representations are fit on training data only, probabilities are isotonic-calibrated on validation, and confidence intervals come from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the test set.

What the ablation says

Pre-call volatility is a strong baseline (0.776 test AUC), because volatility clusters. Text alone reaches 0.674, but adds only about 0.001 AUC on top of the baseline. Voice alone is 0.543 — close to chance — and adding it lowers test AUC from 0.780 to 0.776.

FinBERT mean embeddings reach only 0.553 on their own, below TF-IDF, and they lower the single LightGBM from 0.766 to 0.744. With 1,500 training calls, a 768-d generic vector is too wide for the sample; the sparse lexical representation wins.

Limitations

The label is noisy: five-day realised volatility is affected by macro news unrelated to the call. MAEC transcripts have no speaker labels, so Q&A-specific acoustics are approximated, not isolated. The corpus ends in 2018; modern calls, accents and audio quality may behave differently. This is a research prototype, not investment advice.
